Course Description: 

This course is particularly important for students in the sciences, business and economics.  It is designed to give a thorough introduction to limits, continuity, differentiation and integration of functions.  Differential calculus is the study of instantaneous rates of change of functions and integral calculus is the study of accumulated change and its interpretation as area bounded by the graphs of functions.  Both topics have extensive applications in all fields of science, engineering, business and economics.

 

Course Structure:  

This course uses a flipped classroom format. In a traditional class, new material is presented during class and homework is completed afterward. In a flipped classroom, this process is reversed. Before each class meeting, you are expected to review the assigned instructional materials, includes textbook readings, video lectures, and homework problems. This preparation introduces the fundamental concepts and terminology so that class time can be devoted to developing a deeper understanding of the material.

 

During class, I will focus on answer questions you have about the readings and homework problems.  You will have opportunities to ask questions, collaborate with classmates, and receive feedback.

 

Your success in a flipped classroom depends on completing the assigned preparation before coming to class. Students who arrive prepared are better able to participate in activities, ask meaningful questions, and gain the greatest benefit from our time together. Because class activities build directly on the assigned pre-class work, it is essential that you complete the required preparation before each class meeting.

The flipped classroom is designed to make class time more interactive, engaging, and effective, while helping you develop stronger problem-solving skills and a deeper understanding of the course material.

Course Learning Objectives: 

At the completion of the course a student should be able to,

Demonstrate an understanding of limits of functions including when limits exist and fail to exist.
Identify continuous functions and intervals over which functions are continuous.
Differentiate functions using both limits and differentiation rules.
Solve application problems related to derivatives, such as, velocity and acceleration problems,related rate problems and minimum / maximum Problems.
Graph functions using the first and second derivative tests.
Evaluate antiderivatives of functions and use the antiderivative to evaluate areas bounded by the graphs functions.
Evaluate integrals using substitution techniques.
Approximate integrals using the Trapezoidal and Simpson's rule.

Suggested Study Techniques:

As a guideline I suggest the following steps to study for an exam.  These are of course suggestions and your preferred study techniques may vary.  Just remember, whatever study technique you use study actively. 

 

In a very systematic and organized manner perform the following steps for each section of material covered.  

Review the section and the corresponding lecture notes and write down important points such as definitions, formulas and theorems on a separate sheet of paper or on flash cards.  You can use these to quiz yourself after you have finished studying.
Review typical homework problems covered in class and in the homework.
Last but perhaps the most important step -- put pencil to paper and attempt to do typical homework problems in the same environment and only with the same materials as you are allowed to bring to the exam.  Keep doing this until you are able to correctly answer the majority of problems attempted without looking back at resources (like your textbook or notes) that you are not allowed to bring to the exam (study actively).  Remember, do not simply look back through your notes and say to yourself, "yeah that make sense ... I get that problem ... I understand that point ... I'm ready for this test," without attempting to work out problems for yourself with pencil and paper (don't study passively).

Academic Integrity:

Academic integrity is part of the foundation of an academic community. Any violation of the highest standards of academic honesty threatens the trust upon which an academic community is built and is conduct that violates its fundamental principles. Academic dishonesty is a serious breach of the rules of proper academic conduct. The penalty for the first act of academic dishonesty will be a zero on the piece of work involved or an F in the course, at the discretion of the instructor in consultation with the academic dean as appropriate. A letter describing the incident and the action taken will be sent by the instructor to the student with a copy to the chief academic officer. Academic Affairs will then send a notice to the student and to the Registrar for placement in the student’s file. The letter will serve as a record of a first offense but will be removed from the file upon graduation if no subsequent offense occurs. If a student commits a subsequent offense, the mandatory penalty will be an F in that course and a letter in the file. A student may be dismissed from the College for a second or subsequent offense at the discretion of the chief academic officer, in which case a notation will be placed on the student’s academic record (his or her transcript) noting only the fact of dismissal. For more information, the full policy can be found in the College Catalog.
